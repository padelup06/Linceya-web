Padel Training Plan: A Structure for Your Level

October 23, 2026 · ≈ 7 min read

Random drills feel productive but rarely change your game. A good padel training plan gives each session a job: what to work on, in which order, and how it fits around your week. The plan below starts from what actually happens in your matches. It then splits your time between technique, partner drills, match play and fitness. Pick your level, pick the number of sessions you can realistically keep up, and run it as a short cycle before you reassess.

Start from a diagnosis, not from a drill list

Most players choose drills by habit or by what looks fun on social media. A plan works the other way round. You first need to know what costs you points.

After a match, write down three things:

  • the shot you lost the most points on
  • the situation where you panic (at the net, after a lob, on the back wall)
  • one thing you did well and want to keep

Be specific. “My volleys are bad” is useless. “I miss backhand volleys when the ball comes at my feet” is something you can train.

Video makes this far more reliable, because your memory of a match is biased towards the last few points. Film a full match from behind the back glass, then watch it with a pen in your hand. The four blocks every plan needs

Whatever your level, a balanced cycle mixes four types of work. Skipping one of them is the most common reason players plateau.

Technique. Short, focused work on one shot or one movement: your grip, your preparation, your footwork into the volley. Quality matters more than volume here.

Partner drills. Exercises with one or more players, where the ball comes at a realistic pace and height. This is where a technical fix becomes a reflex.

Match play. Points and sets with a purpose. Play a match while forcing yourself to apply one tactical rule, such as “lob when I am pushed back”.

Physical work. Footwork, agility, legs and core, in a form that supports the court work. For the muscle groups involved, see what muscles padel works.

Your level decides the proportions. Your diagnosis decides the content.

Adapt the plan to your level

Beginner: build a reliable base

Your priority is consistency. Spend most of your time on technique and easy partner rallies: getting the ball back over the net, using the glass, holding a position. Match play stays light and friendly. Add short physical work, mostly movement and balance. Do not rush into smashes or víboras. A solid forehand, backhand and basic volley will win you more games.

Intermediate: fix the leaks

You can rally, but a few weaknesses repeat. This is where a diagnosis pays off most. Give the biggest share of your time to partner drills aimed at your top weakness, such as net play, lobs or defending off the back wall. Use match play to test decisions: where you send the ball, when you move up, when you stay back. Technique work becomes targeted fixes, not general practice.

Advanced: refine and pressure-test

At this level the gains are small and specific. Your plan leans on match play under pressure, tactical patterns with your usual partner, and fine-tuning one or two shots. Physical work matters more, because small improvements in speed and recovery show up in long points. Technique sessions are short and surgical.

Adjust the plan to your available time

The number of sessions you can commit to matters less than doing them consistently. Choose the line that matches your week.

One session a week. Treat it as a mixed session. Warm up with a few minutes of footwork, work on your main weakness through partner drills, then finish with a few points where you apply the same idea. Your regular match counts as your match play, so do not add a second one.

Two sessions a week. Give each session its own focus. One is a training session built around technique and partner drills. The other is a match session with a tactical goal. Fit your physical work into the warm-up of the first.

Three sessions a week. Add a dedicated physical session, or a second technique session if your level is still developing. Keep at least one session as pure match play, so the work transfers to real points.

Run it as a four-week cycle

A plan without an end date drifts. Think in blocks of four weeks:

  1. Week 1: focus on one weakness and one strength to maintain. Keep the drills simple.
  2. Week 2: raise the difficulty. Faster balls, tighter targets, less time to think.
  3. Week 3: bring the work into matches. Give yourself one rule to apply in every game.
  4. Week 4: play more, and review. Film a match and compare it with your starting diagnosis.

Then reassess. If the leak is fixed, move to the next one. If not, repeat the block with a different drill. Do not change the plan every week, because you will never see what works.

FAQ

Can I follow a padel training plan on my own?

Yes. A plan is a structure, not a coach. Choose a weakness from your last match, schedule technique, partner drills and match play around it, and review after four weeks. The weak point of training alone is feedback, which is why filming your sessions helps. If you want to know where a coach still adds value, read whether you really need a padel coach.

How do I know my level before choosing a plan?

Be honest about what happens in a match, not about how long you have played. If you struggle to keep the ball in play, you are a beginner. If you rally well but lose points on the same shots, you are intermediate. If your errors are rare and tactical, you are advanced. When in doubt, start one level lower and move up quickly.

When should I change my padel training plan?

After a full cycle of about four weeks, not before. Compare a recent match with your starting diagnosis. If your main weakness is no longer the biggest source of lost points, pick the next one. If it is still there, keep the same focus but change the drills.

How to keep improving

A good plan is simple: diagnose, choose one weakness, mix technique, partner drills, matches and physical work, then reassess after a few weeks. Keep it short enough that you actually follow it.
