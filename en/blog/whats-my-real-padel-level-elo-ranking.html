What's My Real Padel Level? Understanding the ELO Ranking

July 25, 2026 · ≈ 6 min read

You’ve probably asked yourself this after a match: am I actually decent, or did I just get lucky? Most padel players go years without a clear answer. They guess their level based on vibes, a good day at the club, or what their partner tells them.

That’s where ELO ranking comes in. It’s the same rating logic used in chess for decades, adapted to track how good you really are at padel — match after match, opponent after opponent. No guesswork, just numbers that move based on real results.

Here’s how it works, why it matters, and how to find out where you actually stand.

Why “I think I’m intermediate” isn’t good enough

Ask ten padel players to self-assess and you’ll get ten different definitions of “intermediate.” One person’s intermediate is another’s advanced beginner. Self-assessment is biased by ego, recent memory, and who you happen to play with most.

This matters for practical reasons. If you sign up for a tournament in the wrong category, you’ll either get crushed or bored. If you look for a partner without knowing your real level, you’ll mismatch constantly. And if you’re trying to track progress, “I feel better than last year” isn’t data — it’s a feeling.

A ranking system fixes this by removing the subjectivity. Your level becomes a number, calculated from actual outcomes against actual opponents, not from how confident you felt walking off the court.

How ELO ranking actually works

ELO was invented by Arpad Elo for chess, then adapted to virtually every competitive sport where two sides face off — tennis, football rankings, esports, and now padel.

The principle is simple: every player has a number. After each match, points move from the loser to the winner. But the amount that moves depends on the gap between the two players before the match.

Beat someone way better than you? You gain a lot of points, because the system didn’t expect that outcome.

Beat someone way weaker? You gain almost nothing, because that result was expected.

Lose to someone much weaker? You lose a significant chunk, because that’s a bigger surprise than a loss to a stronger player.

This self-correcting mechanic is what makes ELO more accurate than a simple win/loss tally. Two players can both have a 10-3 record and sit at very different levels, because ELO also asks: who did you beat, and how good were they?

Over time, as you play more matches, your ELO converges toward a number that genuinely reflects your padel level — not your best day, not your worst, but your consistent standard.

Why ELO reacts faster than official categories

Traditional padel categories (often labelled P100, P200, P300, and so on depending on the country) are usually tied to tournament results and get reviewed periodically by federations or clubs. That means your official category can lag behind your real level by weeks or months.

ELO recalculates after every single match. Play a strong weekend and beat two higher-ranked opponents, and your score reflects that almost immediately. This makes ELO particularly useful if you’re improving fast and want feedback that keeps up with you.

What your ELO number actually tells you

A raw ELO score means little on its own — what matters is comparison. Here’s roughly how to read it in a typical padel ranking pool:

  • Low range (beginner): You’re still building consistency. Rallies are short, and results depend heavily on your opponent’s mistakes rather than your own control.
  • Mid range (intermediate): You can sustain rallies, place the ball with some intention, and start reading your opponents’ patterns. This is where most club players sit.
  • Upper-mid range (advanced intermediate): Your shot selection improves, you defend the back glass with more confidence, and net play becomes a real weapon rather than a gamble.
  • High range (advanced/competitive): You consistently beat strong opponents, your tactical decisions hold up under pressure, and your ELO growth slows because there’s less “surprise” left to extract from wins.

The exact thresholds vary depending on the ranking pool and how many players feed into it, so don’t fixate on the absolute number. Focus on the trend: is it climbing, plateauing, or dropping?

How to actually find out your padel level

If you want a real answer instead of a guess, here’s the practical path:

Play ranked or tracked matches consistently. ELO only works with data. A handful of casual games with friends won’t cut it — you need results logged against a range of opponents.

Vary your opponents. Playing the exact same partner or rival every week gives the system little new information. Mixing in different skill levels sharpens the accuracy of your score faster.

Give it time. As mentioned, expect your number to settle after 10-15 matches. Early swings are normal and not a reason to panic or celebrate too early.

Cross-check with technical feedback. ELO tells you what your level is, but not why. If your score is stuck, the missing piece is usually technical: a weak backhand, poor court positioning, or hesitant net play.

FAQ

What is a good padel ELO score for a beginner?

Most brand-new players start somewhere between 1000 and 1200 points, depending on the system’s default. Don’t read too much into the number at first — it’s the trend over your next 10-15 matches that actually tells you something.

How many matches do I need before my ELO becomes reliable?

As a rule of thumb, expect your ranking to stabilise after 10 to 15 competitive matches. Before that, one lucky win or one bad day can swing your score more than it should.

Can my padel level go down?

Yes. ELO is dynamic — it rewards wins against strong opponents and penalises losses against weaker ones. A losing streak, injury layoff, or rusty comeback after months off court will bring your score down.

Is ELO the same as the official padel category (P100, P200, etc.)?

No. Official categories are usually based on tournament results and are updated slowly by federations. ELO is calculated after every match and reacts much faster, which makes it better for tracking week-to-week progress.

Pour progresser concrètement

Your padel level isn’t a feeling — it’s a number that moves based on how you actually perform against real opponents. ELO ranking gives you that clarity, match after match.
