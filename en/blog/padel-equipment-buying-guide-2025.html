Padel Equipment 2025: Racket, Balls & Shoes Buying Guide

Buying padel gear in 2025 is both easier and trickier than ever. Easier because brands now offer rackets and shoes for every level. Trickier because shops push features you don’t actually need at your stage. This guide cuts through the marketing and gives you the real criteria that matter: shape, weight, sole type, ball pressure. Whether you’ve just played your first match or you’re chasing your next category up, you’ll find concrete advice on padel equipment that fits your game, your body and your wallet. No fluff, just what works on court.

How to choose the right padel racket

The racket is your biggest investment, so the choice matters. Three things define a racket: shape, balance and core material.

Shape dictates where the sweet spot sits. Round rackets keep the sweet spot in the centre, which means forgiveness. Teardrop pushes it slightly higher, blending control and power. Diamond puts it near the top for maximum smash power, with very little margin for error.

Balance is about weight distribution. Low balance (handle-heavy) means easy manoeuvrability and faster reactions at the net. High balance (head-heavy) gives more power but tires your arm and shoulder faster. Most intermediate players thrive with a medium balance.

Core material matters too. Soft EVA absorbs impact, protects your elbow and suits learning players. Hard EVA returns more energy but transmits more vibration. Foam cores are softer still, great for comfort but less common in 2025 high-end models.

A good rule: weight should sit between 360 and 380 grams. Lighter feels fragile, heavier kills your wrist after an hour.

Padel balls: pressure, brands and when to replace

Padel balls look like tennis balls but they’re not. They’re slightly smaller, lighter and pressurised lower (around 4.6 to 5.2 kg/cm²). This lower pressure means a controlled bounce off the walls, which is the whole point of the sport.

In 2025, the main certified brands are Bullpadel, Head, Wilson, Adidas and Babolat. The FIP-approved cans are the safest bet if you play in tournaments. For casual sessions, mid-range models last just fine.

How to know when a ball is dead? Drop it from shoulder height onto the court. A fresh ball bounces back to roughly your hip. A worn ball barely reaches your knee. The fuzz also matters: when it gets matted and shiny, aerodynamics suffer and your shots fly unpredictably.

Store balls in a cool, dry place. Heat in a car boot kills pressure within days. Once opened, use them within a month for best results.

Padel shoes: the most underrated piece of gear

Most players overspend on rackets and underspend on shoes. That’s backwards. Bad shoes cause slips, ankle rolls and chronic knee pain. Good shoes save your body and improve your court coverage.

The key is the outsole pattern. Look for:

  • Herringbone (fishbone): best grip on sand-dressed artificial turf, ideal for outdoor courts.
  • Omni / mixed pattern: versatile, works indoor and outdoor, slightly less grippy on heavy sand.
  • Clay-court pattern: avoid for padel, it clogs with sand.

Lateral support is the second priority. Padel involves explosive side-to-side movements. Your shoe needs a reinforced midfoot and a toe bumper to handle drag-step slides.

Cushioning depends on your weight and joints. Heavier players or those over 40 should prioritise gel or air units in the heel. Lighter, faster players can go for low-profile, responsive midsoles.

Try shoes on with the socks you’ll play in, late in the day when feet are slightly swollen. Leave a thumb’s width at the toe.

Matching your gear to your level

Buying expensive padel equipment for a beginner is wasted money. Here’s a realistic level-by-level breakdown.

Beginner (0 to 6 months of play): round-shape racket, soft EVA core, 360-370g, medium-low balance. Mid-range shoes with omni sole. One can of mid-tier balls. Total budget: 200-300 euros.

Intermediate (1 to 3 years): teardrop racket, mixed EVA, 365-375g. Padel-specific shoes with herringbone sole and reinforced toe. Premium balls (FIP-approved). Total budget: 350-500 euros.

Advanced (3+ years, competitive): diamond or teardrop, hard EVA or hybrid, balance tuned to your style. Top-tier shoes, often two pairs rotated. Pro balls. Total budget: 500-900 euros.

Accessories worth buying (and what to skip)

A few extras genuinely improve your padel experience. Others are pure marketing.

Worth buying: - Overgrip: changes the feel of your handle and absorbs sweat. Replace every 5-10 sessions. - Wrist strap: mandatory in most clubs and a safety must. - Padel bag with thermal compartment: protects rackets from car-boot heat that warps frames. - Vibration dampener: optional, but helps if you feel elbow tingles.

Skip or delay: - Smartwatches sold as “padel-specific” rarely deliver useful data. - Heated grips and exotic strings don’t exist in padel (it’s not tennis). - Designer apparel: a regular sports kit works perfectly.

One thing players forget: replace your overgrip before tournaments. A fresh grip costs 4 euros and prevents your racket from spinning in sweaty hands on a key point.

FAQ

How much should I spend on my first padel racket?

For a first racket, a budget of 80 to 150 euros is the sweet spot. Below that, you often get heavy frames with cheap foam that hurt your elbow. Above 200 euros, you’re paying for pro-level stiffness you won’t use yet. Pick a round-shape, soft EVA racket from a known brand. You’ll get comfort, control and enough power to learn properly.

Can I play padel with tennis shoes?

You can, but you really shouldn’t. Tennis shoes for hard court have flat soles that slip on artificial turf, and clay tennis shoes wear down too fast. Padel-specific shoes use a herringbone or mixed sole designed for sand-dressed turf. They give you grip on lateral moves and protect your ankles from the constant direction changes the sport demands.

How long do padel balls actually last?

An unopened pressurised can keeps balls fresh for 12 to 18 months. Once opened, balls lose noticeable pressure after 2 to 3 hours of intense play. Recreational players can stretch a can over 4 or 5 sessions, but competitive players change them every match. If the ball sounds dull and bounces low when dropped from shoulder height, it’s done.

Round, teardrop or diamond racket: which one for me?

Round shape suits beginners and control players: the sweet spot sits in the middle, mistakes are forgiven. Teardrop is the hybrid choice for intermediates wanting balance between power and control. Diamond shape is for advanced players with clean technique and strong shoulders, offering maximum power on smashes but a tiny sweet spot near the top. When in doubt, go round.

Pick your padel equipment with confidence

Good padel equipment matches your level, not your dreams. Start with a forgiving racket, shoes built for sand-dressed turf, and certified balls. Upgrade when your technique outgrows your gear, not before.
