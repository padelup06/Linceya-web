Padel vs Pickleball: Differences, Similarities and Which to Pick

September 8, 2026 · ≈ 5 min read

Both sports exploded in popularity over the last few years, and both get pitched as “easier than tennis.” That’s where the similarities start to blur into confusion. Padel and pickleball share a paddle, a net, and a doubles-friendly format, but the actual experience on court is quite different.

If you’re trying to decide which one to try first, or you’re already torn between the two, this comparison breaks down the real differences: court setup, rules, physical demand, gear cost, and who each sport suits best.

Court and Equipment: Not the Same Game at All

Padel is played on an enclosed court, roughly 20 by 10 meters, surrounded by glass walls and metal mesh. The ball can bounce off these walls, which adds a whole strategic layer you won’t find in pickleball.

Pickleball courts are open, smaller at 13.4 by 6.1 meters, and closer in spirit to a mini tennis or badminton layout. There are no walls, no enclosure, just a net and clearly marked lines including the famous “kitchen” (the non-volley zone).

Equipment differs too. Padel rackets are solid, stringless paddles with holes, paired with a low-pressure ball similar to a tennis ball but softer. Pickleball uses a lighter paddle and a perforated plastic ball, closer to a wiffle ball. Neither sport requires a racket bag full of gear, but padel gear tends to run slightly more expensive.

Rules and Scoring: Familiar Yet Distinct

Padel scoring mirrors tennis almost exactly: 15, 30, 40, game, with sets and match tiebreaks. If you’ve ever played tennis, you’ll feel at home immediately. Serves are underhand, and the ball must bounce once before you can hit it off the wall.

Pickleball has its own scoring system that trips up newcomers. Games are usually played to 11 points, win by 2, and only the serving side can score. Add the “double bounce rule” (both the serve and the return must bounce before volleys are allowed) and the kitchen rule (no volleying inside the non-volley zone), and you’ve got a sport with more built-in constraints than padel.

Both are almost always played in doubles, which keeps the pace social and rallies more strategic than pure power-based.

Physical Demand: Cardio vs Quick Reflexes

This is where the difference between padel and pickleball becomes obvious once you actually play both.

Padel rallies tend to run longer. The walls keep the ball in play, so points often turn into extended exchanges that demand stamina, lateral movement, and repeated sprints to the glass and back. A full padel session leaves most players properly winded.

Pickleball rewards different skills: fast hands, quick reaction time, and precise dinks near the net. Points end faster, movement is more compact, and the overall physical load is lighter, though by no means “easy.” It’s a great low-impact option if you’re managing joint issues or coming back from injury.

Neither sport is inherently “better” for fitness. It depends on what conditioning you’re after: padel leans toward cardio and lateral agility, pickleball leans toward reflexes and hand-eye coordination.

Skill Curve and Social Dynamics

Beginners often find pickleball easier to pick up in the first hour. The court is smaller, the ball moves slower, and basic rallies happen quickly, which makes it a great icebreaker sport for casual groups or first dates with friends.

Padel takes a bit longer to click, mainly because using the walls effectively is counterintuitive at first. New players tend to either avoid the walls entirely or smash the ball straight into them without control. Once it clicks, though, padel opens up a much richer tactical dimension, similar to squash mixed with tennis.

Both sports are deeply social by design. Doubles format, shorter matches, and casual club culture make them easy to fold into a weekly routine with friends or coworkers.

Cost, Access and Where Each Sport Is Growing

Padel is booming across Europe, particularly Spain, France, and Italy, where dedicated clubs with multiple courts are now common in most cities. Court rental usually costs more than pickleball because of the enclosed structure and glass maintenance.

Pickleball has taken off hard in the US and Canada, often using repurposed tennis or basketball courts with painted lines, which keeps access cheap and setup fast. This is part of why it spread so quickly: almost no infrastructure investment needed.

If you’re picking based on where you live, check local club density first. In many European cities, padel courts are easier to book than pickleball ones, and the reverse is often true in North America.

FAQ

Is padel harder than pickleball?

Padel has a steeper technical learning curve because of the walls and doubles positioning, but pickleball demands quick hands and reflexes at the net. Beginners usually feel comfortable on a pickleball court faster, while padel takes longer to click but rewards patience.

Which sport burns more calories, padel or pickleball?

Padel generally burns more calories per session because rallies last longer and the court is bigger, meaning more lateral movement. Pickleball is lower intensity but still a solid cardio workout, especially in fast doubles games.

Can I switch from pickleball to padel easily?

Yes, your hand-eye coordination and net instincts transfer well. The main adjustment is learning to play the ball off the walls and adapting your swing to the longer, heavier padel racket.

Do padel and pickleball use the same court?

No. Padel courts are enclosed with glass and mesh walls and measure 20x10 meters. Pickleball courts are open, smaller (13.4x6.1 meters), and closer in layout to a badminton court.

Which sport is easier on the knees, padel or pickleball?

Pickleball tends to be gentler on joints because of the smaller court and shorter sprints. Padel involves more explosive lateral movement, which can be tougher on knees and ankles if you skip a proper warm-up.

Pour progresser concrètement

There’s no wrong answer between padel and pickleball, only the one that fits your body, your schedule, and what’s available near you. Try both if you can before committing to gear or memberships.
