How to Improve at Padel: 10 Concrete, Measurable Tips

July 22, 2026 · ≈ 6 min read

Most players plateau at intermediate level not because they lack talent, but because they train without a clear target. They play more matches, hit more balls, and somehow stay at the same level for months.

Learning how to improve at padel isn’t about playing harder. It’s about identifying specific, measurable weaknesses and drilling them on purpose. Here are 10 concrete tips you can apply this week, each one tied to something you can actually track.

1. Track Your Net Position, Not Just Your Shots

Amateur players lose most points because they’re stuck too far behind the net, not because of bad technique.

The fix is simple: after every point, check where you were standing when you lost it. If you were more than a metre behind the service line, that’s your leak.

Set a measurable goal: aim to reach the net within the first three shots of 80% of your rallies. Count it over your next five matches.

2. Count Your Unforced Errors by Shot Type

Most players know they make errors. Few know which shot causes them.

Grab a notebook or use your phone between games and log every unforced error with one word: bandeja, smash, wall, volley. After three matches, one shot will stand out.

That’s your priority drill for the next two weeks. Improvement at padel is rarely about everything at once — it’s about fixing the one leak that’s bleeding the most points.

3. Master the Lob Before Anything Else

The lob is the most underrated shot in padel and the fastest way to buy time and reset a point. Yet most amateurs only use it defensively, out of panic.

Practice it as an offensive tool: aim for the back glass, medium height, landing within the last two metres of the court. Do 20 reps per session against a wall or with a partner feeding balls.

Measurable target: land 15 out of 20 lobs in that zone. Once you hit that consistently, your rally patience — and your win rate — will jump.

4. Fix Your Bandeja Before You Chase the Smash

Everyone wants a killer smash. Almost nobody has a reliable bandeja, which is the shot that actually controls the point.

The bandeja isn’t about power — it’s about control and depth, keeping your opponents pinned back. Drill it slow, focusing on a flat, controlled trajectory rather than trying to finish the point.

Track it simply: out of 10 bandejas, how many land past the service line on the other side? Aim for 7 out of 10 before you even think about upgrading your smash.

5. Play More Points From the Back Right Position

If you’re right-handed and always play left, or vice versa, you’re only training half your game. Positional flexibility is a real, measurable skill.

Spend 20 minutes per session playing from your weaker side. Track your comfort level from 1 to 5 after each session and watch the number climb over a month.

This single change often unlocks tactical options you didn’t know you had, especially in doubles where communication and coverage matter as much as shot quality.

6. Reduce Your Reaction Time at the Net

Net exchanges are won and lost in fractions of a second. Most amateur players react late because they’re not in a ready position between shots.

Drill volleys with a partner feeding fast, close-range balls. Track how many out of 20 you return cleanly without stepping back.

A ready position — knees bent, racket up, weight forward — is the single biggest fix here. It costs nothing and it’s purely a habit, so measure it every session until it becomes automatic.

7. Set a Serve Consistency Target

The serve in padel doesn’t need to be a weapon, but it does need to be reliable. A double fault or a weak serve handed straight to the opponent’s smash costs free points every match.

Pick a target zone — body height, into the T — and hit 20 serves. Count how many land where you want them to land.

Improving at padel often comes down to removing free points for your opponents before adding new weapons for yourself. A consistent serve does exactly that.

8. Analyze Your Matches, Not Just Your Drills

Drilling in isolation is useful, but it doesn’t tell you what actually happens under match pressure. That’s where most players skip a step.

Filming a match and reviewing it afterwards reveals patterns you’d never notice live — where you stand too flat-footed, which side you avoid hitting to, how often you rush a shot you didn’t need to rush.

9. Build a Two-Week Drill Cycle, Not a Random One

Random practice feels productive but rarely moves the needle. Structure beats volume.

Pick one weakness from your error log, drill it for two weeks with a specific measurable target, then reassess. Move to the next weakness once you’ve hit 70-80% consistency on the current one.

This cycle — identify, drill, measure, move on — is how club players actually climb levels instead of plateauing for a season.

10. Track Your Match Results Against Similar-Level Opponents

Improvement is hard to see day to day. It’s much clearer over 10 or 15 matches against players of a similar level.

Keep a simple log: date, opponent level, result, and one thing you did better than last time. Patterns emerge fast — you’ll notice which tactical adjustments actually translate into wins.

A certified ranking system, like an ELO-based one, makes this even easier since it gives you an objective number that moves as your level moves, rather than relying on memory or gut feeling.

FAQ

How long does it take to improve at padel?

Most amateur players notice real technical progress within 6 to 8 weeks of consistent, focused practice. Tactical improvement takes longer, usually 3 to 6 months, because it depends on match experience and reading your opponents.

What’s the fastest way to improve at padel?

Fix your positioning and shot selection before working on power. Most amateur mistakes come from being in the wrong spot or forcing a shot that isn’t there, not from lacking technique.

Do I need a coach to improve at padel?

A coach speeds things up but isn’t mandatory. Filming your matches, tracking specific stats, and drilling with purpose can get you most of the way there on your own.

How often should I play to improve at padel?

Two to three sessions a week is the sweet spot for amateur players. One of those sessions should be dedicated drilling, not just casual matches, so you actually fix weaknesses instead of repeating them.

Pour progresser concrètement

Improving at padel comes down to measuring the right things and drilling them on purpose, not playing more casual matches and hoping for the best. Pick one or two tips from this list, track them for two weeks, and reassess.
