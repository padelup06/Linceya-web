How to Be More Consistent in Padel: Fewer Errors

November 1, 2026 · ≈ 8 min read

You win the rallies you should lose and lose the ones you should win. Most of the points slipping away are your own: a volley into the tape, a bandeja that dies in the net, a lob that lands short. If you want to be more consistent in padel, the answer is rarely “hit softer”. It is a mix of margin, shot choice and energy management, plus an honest look at where your points actually go. Here is how to work on each, step by step.

Consistency is not playing it safe

Consistent players are not passive. They hit the same quality of shot at the end of a set as at the start, under pressure, against a good opponent.

That changes what you work on. The goal is not to avoid risk but to take risks that pay off. A well-aimed chiquita at the feet of a net player is a smart risk. A flat smash from the back of the court at a ball that is too low is a coin flip.

This article is written for players who already have the basics: you get the ball over, you know where to stand, you can hold a rally. If grip, court position and the first rules still feel shaky, start with the padel beginner mistakes guide, then come back.

Diagnose your match: sort the points you lose

You cannot fix “I make too many mistakes” because it is too vague. Break it down first.

Film your next match from behind the back glass, or ask a friend to. Then watch it and note down only the points you lost. Put each one in a single box:

  • Direct error: you missed an easy ball with no pressure on you.
  • Shot choice: the ball was playable, but you picked a shot that was too ambitious for the situation.
  • Positioning: you were late, too deep or too far from your partner, so the shot was forced.
  • Opponent level: they hit a genuinely good ball and you had no real answer.

Be strict with the last box. Most players overuse it. A tough ball is one that would trouble players at your own level, not one that was merely fast.

After a match or two, a pattern appears. Many intermediate players find that most lost points sit in “shot choice” and “positioning”, not in raw technique. That tells you to train decisions, not just strokes. Build margin over the net and inside the court

Most unforced errors come from aiming too close to a limit: the top of the net, the sideline, the corner of the glass. Give yourself room.

Aim higher over the net. On neutral balls, think of a target about an arm’s length above the tape. A ball that clears it comfortably gives you depth, and a deep ball is harder to attack. A ball that skims the tape is one bad contact away from the net.

Aim at big targets. In the middle of the court, a ball hit to the centre between the opponents creates doubt and removes the sideline from the equation. Save the tight angles for balls you are well set up for.

Hit through the ball, not at it. Many errors come from stopping the swing at contact. Finish the stroke, keep the racket head moving forward, and let the ball leave your strings with a smooth trajectory.

Use the walls to your advantage. A ball that bounces and rebounds off the back glass buys you time. Let it come off the wall instead of rushing to take it early, especially when you are slightly out of position. Choose the shot that fits the ball

Shot choice is where intermediate players leak the most points. Ask yourself one question before every ball: what is the most probable way to keep the point or win it from here?

Some simple reference points:

  • Below net height and behind the service line: keep it neutral. A deep lob, a ball down the middle or a controlled return off the wall.
  • High ball in your attacking zone: a vibora or bandeja keeps you in control. Keep the smash for balls you can hit cleanly with your feet set.
  • Opponents at the net, you at the back: the lob is your reset button. A defensive lob that reaches the back third gives you time to recover your position.
  • Ball at your feet: slow it down. A soft block or a chiquita beats a hard drive into the tape.

The pattern is the same everywhere: match the ambition of the shot to the quality of the ball you received. When you are out of position, you are allowed to play a boring ball. It keeps the rally alive.

Keep your legs and your head fresh late in sets

Errors rarely come out of nowhere. They pile up when your legs get heavy and your concentration slips, usually in the second half of a long set.

Tired legs mean late preparation, and late preparation means rushed swings. Watch your own footwork in the video: do you still take small adjustment steps in the final games, or do you start reaching for the ball?

Some habits help:

  • Prepare early. Racket back as soon as you read the ball, not when it arrives.
  • Use the changeovers. Drink, breathe slowly and decide on one simple focus for the next games, such as “high over the net”.
  • Slow the point down on purpose. After a long rally, a lob buys you a few seconds of recovery.
  • Reset after a mistake. Pick a small routine, like a breath and a glance at your partner, so one error does not turn into three.

Make it routine between matches

Consistency is built in repetition with intention. A few short blocks beat one chaotic session.

Try a simple structure: a rally exercise with a partner where the only target is to keep the ball above net height for as many balls as you can, then a block of bandejas or volleys aimed at a clear zone, then points played with one constraint, such as no winners before the sixth ball. Decide your focus before each block, not after.

Then close the loop. Film another match after a few weeks and sort your lost points again. If the “shot choice” box shrinks and your errors move to a different box, the work is paying off. For a full method that does not depend on a coach, see how to improve at padel without a coach.

FAQ

Why do I lose at padel even when I feel I play well?

Usually because your own errors give away more points than your opponents win. Many players remember their best shots and forget the easy balls they missed. Film a match and sort every lost point: direct error, shot choice, positioning or a genuinely better opponent. The results often point to decisions and positioning more than to technique.

How do I stop hitting the ball into the net?

Raise your target and finish your swing. Aim about an arm’s length above the tape on neutral balls, and keep the racket moving forward through contact. Also check your preparation: if you start the stroke late, you tend to push the ball with a short, low swing, and the net takes it.

Should I play slower to be more consistent?

Not necessarily. Slower balls with no purpose are easy for opponents to attack. Consistency comes from controlled pace, good depth and balls placed where they are hard to attack. Reduce power when you are out of position or under pressure, and increase it only on balls you are set up to hit.

Can fatigue really cause unforced errors?

Yes. Tired legs slow your preparation, and a late swing is a rushed swing. Notice whether your errors cluster at the end of sets. If they do, work on early preparation, use changeovers to reset, and play a higher lob after long rallies to recover your breath.

How to keep improving

Consistency comes from knowing where your points go and training that specific habit. Sort your lost points, add margin over the net, and match every shot to the ball you receive.
