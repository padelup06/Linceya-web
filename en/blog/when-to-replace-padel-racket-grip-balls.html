When to Replace Your Padel Racket, Grip or Balls

August 30, 2026 · ≈ 6 min read

You’ve probably felt it: a shot that should’ve landed clean flies long, or your grip slips at the worst moment. Before you blame your technique, check your gear. Rackets, grips and balls all degrade quietly, and most players wait way too long to swap them out.

This guide breaks down the exact signs that tell you it’s time to replace each piece of equipment, so you’re not losing points to something as fixable as a worn-out overgrip.

Signs Your Padel Racket Needs Replacing

A padel racket doesn’t announce its death with a dramatic snap. It fades gradually, and that’s exactly why so many players miss the warning signs.

Cracks and delamination. Check the frame and the face under good light. Hairline cracks often start at the edges or around the holes. If you see any, stop using it. A racket that breaks mid-swing can seriously hurt your wrist or elbow.

A change in sound. Hit a few balls and listen. A healthy racket produces a crisp, solid “pop.” A dying one sounds duller, almost hollow. That shift usually means the core material (EVA or foam) has started breaking down internally, even if the surface looks fine.

Loss of power and control. If shots that used to land deep are now falling short, or your smashes feel weaker despite the same swing, your racket has likely lost some of its responsiveness. This is one of the hardest signs to notice because it happens gradually, over months.

Visible wear on the face. Sanded-down textures, faded paint in the sweet spot, or a rougher-than-usual surface all affect spin and ball grip. If your slices and effects feel less sharp than they used to, take a close look at the racket face.

As a rule of thumb, a racket played 2-3 times a week for a year is due for an inspection. Competitive players pushing 4+ sessions a week should think about replacing every 12 months, sometimes sooner.

When to Change Your Padel Grip

The grip is the cheapest, most overlooked piece of padel equipment, and also the one that affects your game the fastest when it’s worn out.

It feels slippery, especially when you sweat. This is the number one sign. A fresh grip has tack; it should stick slightly to your palm. Once that tackiness disappears, your hand has to work harder to hold the racket steady, which affects both power and precision.

Visible wear patterns. Look for shiny spots, thinning material, or grooves where your fingers naturally rest. These patches lose cushioning first, and once they’re compromised, your whole grip feel changes.

You’re adjusting your hold mid-point. If you catch yourself re-gripping or shifting your hand position during a rally, that’s often a sign the surface has become inconsistent rather than a technique issue.

Timeframe: an overgrip typically lasts 4 to 8 hours of actual play. If you train twice a week for an hour, that’s roughly every 2 to 4 weeks. Players who sweat heavily or play in hot climates should check weekly.

Unlike rackets and balls, grips are a low-cost fix. There’s no real reason to play with a bad one when a new overgrip costs a few euros and takes two minutes to install.

How to Know When Padel Balls Are Dead

Padel balls are pressurized, and pressure is exactly what disappears first, long before the felt looks worn.

The bounce test. Drop the ball from about a meter onto a hard padel court surface. A fresh ball should bounce back to roughly 70% of the drop height. If it barely reaches half that, the ball has lost too much pressure to play well.

Felt condition. Fuzzy, worn felt slows the ball down and changes how it grips the racket face on slices and effects. Smooth, bald patches are an obvious sign it’s time to swap the set.

Inconsistent behavior between balls. If some balls in your tube feel lively and others feel dead, that’s a clear signal the set has aged unevenly, which happens once a tube has been opened and exposed to air for a while.

Session count. A can of balls used 2-3 times a week for casual play usually holds decent pressure for 3 to 5 sessions. Tournament or high-level play often calls for a fresh set every match, since ball behavior directly affects timing and shot depth.

Storage matters too. Balls left in a hot car or direct sun lose pressure much faster than balls kept at room temperature. If you store your tube properly between sessions, you’ll get noticeably more life out of it.

Why Equipment Condition Affects Your Game More Than You Think

It’s tempting to think gear problems are minor compared to technique issues, but the two are more connected than most players assume.

A dead racket forces you to swing harder to get the same power, which throws off your timing and often leads to compensating with bad form. A slippery grip makes your shots less consistent, because your hand position subtly shifts point to point. Flat balls change the rhythm of every rally, since they arrive slower and bounce lower than expected.

If you’re working on improving specific parts of your game — placement, timing, shot depth — worn equipment can mask real progress or create false negatives. You might be doing everything right and still missing shots simply because the ball you’re using has lost half its bounce.

FAQ

How often should I replace my padel racket?

Most club players should replace their racket every 1 to 2 years with regular play, or sooner if you notice cracks, a dull sound on impact, or a drop in power. Competitive players who train several times a week often replace theirs yearly.

Can a cracked padel racket still be used?

You can technically still hit with it, but a cracked racket loses structural integrity fast. Cracks spread under repeated impact, and a sudden full break mid-swing is a real risk to your wrist. Replace it as soon as you spot one.

How long does a padel grip actually last?

A padel overgrip lasts about 4 to 8 hours of play before it loses tackiness, depending on how much you sweat. Casual players might change it monthly, while regular players often replace it every one to two weeks.

Why do padel balls go flat so quickly?

Padel balls lose internal pressure with every hit, and altitude, heat and storage conditions speed this up. A set used two to three times a week typically needs replacing after 3 to 5 sessions to keep bounce consistent.

Does bad equipment actually affect performance?

Yes, more than most players realize. A worn grip changes your hold and control, a dead racket cuts your power, and flat balls throw off your timing. Fixing these three things often improves your game faster than a new technique.

Pour progresser concrètement

Checking your racket, grip and balls regularly takes five minutes and can save you from months of frustrating, unexplained bad form. Build it into your routine: inspect your racket monthly, your grip weekly, and your balls before every serious session.
