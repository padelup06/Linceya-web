When Did Padel Arrive in France? The Full Timeline

September 20, 2026 · ≈ 6 min read

If you’ve only started hearing about padel in the last few years, you’re not imagining things. The sport has been in France far longer than most people think, but it stayed under the radar for decades before suddenly taking off. So when did padel arrive in France, exactly, and why did it take so long to catch on?

The Early Years: Padel’s Quiet Arrival in the 1980s

Padel first showed up in France in the 1980s, mostly in the south, close to the Spanish border where the sport was already established. A handful of clubs in Aquitaine and around the Basque Country built the first courts, largely thanks to cross-border influence from Spain.

At this stage, padel was a curiosity rather than a movement. Courts were rare, equipment was hard to find, and most French sports clubs had never even heard the word “padel.”

Tennis dominated the racket sport landscape completely, and there was little incentive for clubs to invest in something so unfamiliar. Padel existed in France, technically, but it stayed a niche activity for the next twenty years.

The Slow Build: 2000s to Mid-2010s

Through the 2000s, padel grew at a snail’s pace. A few more courts appeared, mostly attached to existing tennis clubs looking to diversify, but there was no coordinated push to promote the sport nationally.

The French Tennis Federation (FFT) took over the governance of padel in France in 2010, which gave the sport some structure for the first time. This mattered because it meant official rules, referee training, and a path toward organised competition.

Still, growth stayed modest. By 2015, France had only a few hundred courts nationwide, concentrated heavily in the south-west. Compare that to Spain’s tens of thousands of courts at the time, and it’s clear France was years behind its neighbour.

Awareness was the biggest barrier. Most French players simply didn’t know padel existed as an option, and those who tried it often had to travel far to find a court.

The Turning Point: 2016-2019

Something shifted around 2016. A new wave of private investors began building dedicated padel clubs instead of relying on tennis facilities to add a court or two as an afterthought.

Paris and other major cities started seeing their first standalone padel venues. This mattered because it moved padel out of its regional stronghold and into places where a much bigger population could try it.

Media coverage helped too. As professional padel tournaments gained visibility across Europe, French sports outlets started covering the sport more seriously, introducing it to audiences who had never picked up a paddle.

By 2019, France had roughly 500 courts, still small compared to Spain but a clear signal that momentum was building. Clubs that had been hesitant to invest now saw enough demand to justify construction.

The Explosion: 2020 Onwards

This is where the real story of padel’s growth in France begins. Between 2020 and today, the number of courts has multiplied several times over, and padel has gone from a curiosity to one of the fastest-growing sports in the country.

A few things drove this surge:

  • Accessibility: Padel is easier to pick up than tennis for beginners, since the smaller court and enclosed walls forgive mistakes that would end a tennis rally instantly.
  • Social appeal: Doubles-only format means every game is played with friends, which fits perfectly with how younger adults want to socialise through sport.
  • Investment: Private padel-only clubs opened across nearly every region, no longer limited to the south-west.
  • Word of mouth: Players who tried padel once often got hooked fast, and they brought friends along.

By the mid-2020s, France counted several thousand courts, with new venues opening at a pace that outstrips almost any other racket sport in Europe. Cities like Paris, Lyon, Marseille, and Toulouse now have dense networks of clubs, and smaller towns are catching up quickly.

Tennis clubs that resisted the trend for years are now adding padel courts simply to keep members from leaving for dedicated padel venues nearby.

Why the Late Start Actually Helped

It might seem odd, but France’s slow start with padel arguably worked in its favour. By the time the sport exploded here, other countries had already worked out best practices for club design, coaching structures, and competition formats.

French clubs didn’t have to reinvent anything. They copied what worked in Spain and Argentina, skipped years of trial and error, and built modern facilities from day one.

This is part of why so many new French players progress quickly. Good coaching, clear structure, and a supportive club culture were in place almost immediately, rather than developing organically over decades the way it did in padel’s home countries.

FAQ

When did padel arrive in France?

Padel arrived in France in the 1980s through the south, near the Spanish border, but it stayed marginal for decades. Real growth only started around 2015-2016, and the sport truly exploded after 2019.

Why did padel take so long to grow in France?

France already had a strong tennis culture and infrastructure, which slowed padel’s adoption. Court construction costs, limited awareness, and no major federation push before the 2010s also held it back.

How many padel courts are there in France now?

France went from a few dozen courts in the early 2010s to several thousand today, with new clubs opening every month across nearly every region, especially in the south and around major cities.

Is padel more popular than tennis in France now?

Not yet in terms of total players, but padel is growing far faster in relative terms. Many tennis clubs are converting or adding padel courts because demand keeps rising year after year.

What sparked the padel boom in France after 2019?

A mix of factors: Spanish and South American expats spreading the sport, private investors building dedicated clubs, media coverage of professional tournaments, and word of mouth from players who found it easier to pick up than tennis.
