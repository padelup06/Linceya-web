When to Move to the Net in Padel: Perfect Timing to Dominate

July 19, 2026 · ≈ 7 min read

Every padel player has felt it: you rush the net at the wrong moment, get lobbed, and spend the next three shots scrambling backwards. Knowing when to move to the net in padel isn’t about speed — it’s about reading the point correctly and moving as a pair, not as an individual sprinting forward.

Get the timing right and you turn defence into control. Get it wrong and you hand your opponents an easy point. This piece breaks down the exact cues that tell you it’s time to advance, the mistakes that ruin good positioning, and how to train that instinct until it becomes automatic.

Why Net Position Decides the Point in Padel

Padel is won and lost at the net. The team that controls that zone controls the angles, the pace, and ultimately the point. From the net, you can volley aggressively, cut off angles, and put pressure on your opponents before they can reset.

The problem is that most amateur players treat the net as a fixed destination rather than a moment to be earned. They walk forward regardless of what just happened in the rally, which means they arrive at the worst possible time — right as their opponents are about to lob or hit a passing shot.

Good net position isn’t about being close to the glass. It’s about being there when your shot has earned you the right to be there. That distinction changes everything about how you move.

The Core Signal: Your Shot Quality

The single clearest rule for when to move to the net in padel is this: advance after a shot that puts your opponents in trouble, not before.

A few concrete triggers:

  • A clean volley that lands deep or at their feet. If you volley well, follow it forward immediately.
  • A well-executed bandeja that pushes them back towards their own baseline. This is one of the most common net-approach shots in modern padel.
  • A lob that forces them to retreat. If your opponents have to run back to defend a lob, that’s your cue to claim the net.
  • A weak return from your opponents that lands short or with no pace. Short balls are invitations — take them.

Conversely, if your own shot is defensive, rushed, or lacks depth, don’t move up yet. Staying back one more shot and resetting is almost always smarter than advancing on a shaky ball.

Reading Your Opponents’ Body Position

Your shot quality is half the equation. The other half is watching what your opponents are doing, because their body position tells you what shot is coming next.

Signs it’s safe to move forward:

  • They’re stretching or off-balance to reach the ball.
  • Their racket is low, suggesting a defensive or lifted shot rather than an aggressive one.
  • They’re running backwards, which almost always precedes a lob rather than a passing shot.

Signs to hold your position:

  • They’re set, balanced, and facing the net with time to prepare.
  • Their racket is up high, which often signals an incoming lob over your head — if you’re already moving in, you’ll be caught flat-footed.
  • One of them is close to the net themselves, meaning a quick exchange is more likely than a defensive shot.

Reading these cues takes repetition. Watching match footage of yourself — where you can pause the moment before you moved up and check what your opponent was actually doing — speeds up this learning curve enormously. Moving Together: The Pair Synchronisation Problem

Individual timing means nothing if your partner doesn’t move with you. One of the most common structural mistakes in amateur padel is a gap opening between partners — one player at the net, the other stuck three metres back near the service line.

This gap is exactly where good opponents will aim a lob. It creates confusion about who covers it, and by the time someone reacts, the point is lost.

The fix is simple in principle, harder in practice: move on the same shot, every time. If your partner hits the trigger shot (a strong volley, bandeja, or deep lob), you both step forward together. If neither of you hit a strong shot, you both hold your ground.

Some practical habits that help:

  • Call it out loud in training — “up” or “back” — until it becomes instinctive.
  • Watch your partner’s split step, not just the ball. A balanced partner is a partner ready to advance.
  • Practice the specific transition from mid-court to net as a pair drill, not just individually.

Teams that move as a unit close gaps automatically. Teams that don’t will always have a weak seam that smart opponents exploit within the first few games.

Common Timing Mistakes That Cost Points

A few recurring errors show up constantly, even among intermediate players who otherwise have solid technique:

Moving up on a neutral shot. Not every decent return earns a net position. If the ball you hit was merely “okay” — not deep, not angled, not forcing anything — advancing invites a passing shot or lob.

Freezing at the service line. This no-man’s-land is the worst place to stand in padel. You’re too far from the net to volley aggressively and too close to the baseline to defend a lob comfortably. When you decide to move, commit fully to the net rather than stalling halfway.

Ignoring the recovery lob. After you’ve been pushed back by an aggressive shot, resist the urge to sprint forward again immediately. Recover your position first, then look for the next real opportunity to advance.

Not adjusting for surface speed or wind. Faster courts and windy conditions change how much time a lob gives you to retreat. On these days, hold the net position a fraction longer before you’re fully certain of your recovery ability.

Fixing these patterns usually isn’t about raw athleticism — it’s about recognising the moment and reacting a half-second sooner, which comes from repetition and honest feedback on your own play.

FAQ

Is it always better to be at the net in padel?

Not always. Being at the net is only an advantage if you get there with a controlled ball and good court coverage. Rushing forward on a weak lob or a deep shot from your opponents leaves you exposed to a lob over your head or a smash. Position matters more than proximity to the net.

What shot should trigger my move to the net?

A solid volley, a well-placed bandeja, or any shot that pushes your opponents back and forces them to play defensively. If your shot lands short or lacks depth, stay back a beat longer and wait for a better opportunity.

How do I know if my partner is ready to move up with me?

Watch their split step and shoulders. If they’re balanced and facing the net after their shot, they’re ready. If they’re still recovering from a stretch or off-balance return, call out to hold the line together before advancing.

What’s the biggest mistake amateurs make with net timing?

Moving up too early, before the ball that triggers the advance has actually landed well. This creates a gap between partners and leaves the net player vulnerable to a lob. The fix is training yourself to move on the bounce of your own good shot, not before.
